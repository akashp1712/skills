## LinkedIn

> At Amazon, when something broke, the question was never "who messed up."
>
> It was: **do we have a mechanism in place so it doesn't happen again?**
>
> The logic is uncomfortable and correct. People already had good intentions when the problem happened. So intentions were never the variable worth changing.
>
> There is an old adage there: good intentions don't work, mechanisms do.
>
> Solo founders have more good intentions than anyone, and no mechanisms at all.
>
> Five that survive when the team is one person:
>
> → **Velocity** — decide at 70% of the information you wish you had
> → **Single-thread** — two open threads run at 35% each, not 50%
> → **Re-entry** — recover context in two minutes, not ninety
> → **Input metrics** — measure what you control, not MRR you can't move
> → **Compounding** — never solve the same problem twice
>
> The one nobody expects:
>
> Bezos wrote in the 2016 shareholder letter that without escalation, the default dispute resolution mechanism is exhaustion. Whoever has more stamina carries the decision.
>
> Work alone and both parties are you. The winner is the anxious voice at midnight that wants to rebuild instead of ship.
>
> Writing the decision down is what ends the argument, because it cannot keep circling once both sides are on the page.
>
> I packaged all five as a free agent skill. It writes real state files — a decision log, a quality bar, a handoff note — instead of giving advice.
>
> npx skills add akashp1712/skills --skill team-of-one
>
> MIT. Take it.
